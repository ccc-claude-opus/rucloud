## Overview

**Social structure** is the relatively stable order formed by the way institutions and roles in a society are linked. It is a basic unit of sociological analysis, used to explain how resources, power and opportunity are distributed unevenly among people.

## Concept and levels of analysis

Social structure differs from social event: a strike is an event, a collective bargaining regime is a structure. Marx argued in the Grundrisse that the relations of production form the skeleton of civil society, upon which the legal and political superstructure is built. Structural analysis therefore asks not only who holds what but how institutions connect: schools link family to labour market, firms link capital to labour, and the state adjusts the rest through taxation and regulation.

## Three theoretical orientations

| Orientation | Focus | Representative figures |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Structural functionalism | How institutions maintain integration and order | Durkheim, Parsons, Merton |
| Social conflict theory | How inequality in power and resources reproduces itself | Marx, Weber, Dahrendorf |
| Symbolic interactionism | How meanings form and are negotiated in interaction | Mead, Blumer, Goffman |

## Class and forms of capital

Weber separated hierarchy into economic, social and cultural capital as partly independent dimensions, noting that a Marxian class captures only one of them. Bourdieu, in Distinction of 1979, showed that capitals convert into one another at differing exchange rates: economic capital converts into cultural capital through education and taste, and cultural capital back into economic capital, but not evenly. High cultural capital does not guarantee high income, nor the reverse, which explains why earnings and schooling overlap only partially.

## Measuring inequality

The Gini coefficient $G = \frac{\sum_{i}\sum_{j}|x_i - x_j|}{2n^2}$ runs from 0 to 1, with higher values meaning more concentrated income; Taiwan's ratio of the top to bottom income quintiles has recently stood between 1.9 and 2.0 times, above the level in most OECD countries. Social mobility rates are also cited: high intergenerational mobility means background matters less for outcomes, though differences in educational and occupational attainment are often more pronounced than pure class reproduction.
