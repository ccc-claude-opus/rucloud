## Overview

**Drama** is literature written for performance, realized through actors’ dialogue and action before an audience. It is at once a script and a constrained narrative technique: time, setting, and available personnel are all limited.

## Elements and plot

In the Poetics, Aristotle names six elements of tragedy — plot, character, thought, diction, music, and spectacle — and makes plot the core, insisting on a single unified action and rejecting the juxtaposition of two plots. The three-act structure now standard in modern theatre divides a play of roughly $120$ minutes into three acts of about $40$ minutes each.

## The three-act structure

The proportions are not law: classical tragedy often resists three acts, organizing itself into fixed episodes interlaced with choral and dance passages. Still, since the twentieth century the three-act scheme has become the shared vocabulary of drama schools, letting scripts from different cultures be compared and giving directors and actors a common baseline.

| Act | Share | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Act I | ~25 % | Establishes time, place, relationships; opens the central conflict |
| Act II | ~50 % | Escalates the conflict through obstacle and choice |
| Act III | ~25 % | Climax and resolution, or a deliberately open ending |

## Traditions and venues

Performance conditions have long shaped dramatic form. The theatre at Epidaurus held roughly $15,000$ spectators, a scale at which actors had to project, and carefully designed tragedy was staged there. In China, each Yuan zaju play was divided into $4$ acts, each in a single gong mode, making musical and emotional registers mutually constraining.

| Tradition | Structural feature | Representative playwright |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Classical Greek tragedy | Choral sections drive emotion and argument | Aeschylus |
| Yuan zaju | Four acts per play, one gong mode each | Guan Hanqing |
| Kunqu opera | Performed in detached numbered scenes | Tang Xianzu |
| Modern prose drama | Plain dialogue without musical interludes | Chekhov |
