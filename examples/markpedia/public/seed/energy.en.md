## Overview

**Energy** is the capacity to do work and change the state of a system, and it is the central conserved quantity in physics. Energy takes forms such as kinetic, potential, internal, electrical and chemical energy, converting from one to another without ever being destroyed.

## Forms of energy and their formulas

Work and energy share an origin: the work done by a force $F$ over a displacement $s$ takes the form $W = F s \cos\theta$ in one dimension. The common forms are summarised below.

| Form | Formula | Typical magnitude |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Kinetic | $E_k = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$ | A 70 kg runner at $10$ m/s stores about $3500$ J |
| Gravitational potential | $E_p = mgh$ | Lifting $1$ kg by $1$ m costs about $9.8$ J |
| Chemical | — | Burning $1$ kg of coal releases about $3 \times 10^7$ J |
| Nuclear | $\Delta m c^2$ | Fission of $1$ kg of uranium-235 yields about $8 \times 10^{13}$ J |

Power measures how fast energy is converted, defined by $P = \frac{dE}{dt}$ and measured in watts (W); $1$ W equals $1$ joule per second and $1$ kWh is about $3.6 \times 10^6$ J.

## Conservation and conversion efficiency

Total energy is conserved in an isolated system, so energy can only change form. No heat engine converts heat entirely into work, and the Carnot efficiency gives the ceiling $\eta = 1 - \frac{T_c}{T_h}$ where temperatures must be absolute in kelvin, so reservoirs at $300$ K and $600$ K cap efficiency at $50$ %. Global annual energy consumption is on the order of $6 \times 10^{20}$ J, still dominated by fossil fuels.

## Storage and supply

Energy supply divides into three families, each with its own storage form and limitations.

- **Fossil fuels**: high energy density and easy transport, but carbon emissions are the main drawback.
- **Nuclear power**: the mass defect of uranium-235 provides far more energy per kilogram than coal, with about $200$ MeV released per fission.
- **Renewables**: solar and wind are inexhaustible sources, but output varies with weather and is usually paired with storage.

Lithium-ion batteries are the dominant storage device in electric vehicles, with energy densities near $250$ to $300$ Wh/kg, so a $60$ kWh traction pack weighs roughly $200$ to $240$ kg of cells.
