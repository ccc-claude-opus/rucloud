## Overview

**Ecology** studies interactions among organisms and between organisms and their environment, spanning levels from individuals and populations to ecosystems and the biosphere. It explains the rules governing energy flow, nutrient cycling and species coexistence.

## Levels of study

Zoologist Ernst Haeckel introduced the term Ökologie in 1866 to describe the relations between organisms and their surroundings. The field then expanded from individual ecology to populations, communities and ecosystems, and on to the biosphere and landscape scales. Population ecology describes mortality patterns with survivorship curves and life tables, while ecosystem ecology characterizes system operation through energy flow and nutrient cycling, together forming the backbone of modern ecology.

## Energy flow and trophic levels

Energy enters an ecosystem when producers fix it by photosynthesis and then passes along food chains. Lindeman's law of energy flow puts transfer efficiency between trophic levels at only about $10$ %, which is why energy pyramids are always broad at the base and narrow at the top. Global primary production amounts to roughly $105$ billion tonnes of carbon a year, and although the ocean covers $71$ % of the planet's surface it contributes close to half of that production.

| Trophic level | Transfer efficiency | Typical organisms |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Producers | — | Algae, grasses, phytoplankton |
| Primary consumers | ~10 % | Herbivorous insects, zooplankton |
| Secondary consumers | ~10 % | Predatory insects, small fish |
| Decomposers | Return nutrients as inorganic | Bacteria, fungi |

## The biosphere and species diversity

The biosphere is the total range of life on Earth, spanning forests, oceans, grasslands, wetlands and tundra. Forest now covers about $4.06$ billion hectares, some $31$ % of the land surface, and the number of species is estimated at around $8.7$ million, of which only some $2$ million have been recorded. Diversity peaks in the tropics and along coasts and falls off toward the poles and the deep sea, a pattern reflecting both energy supply and evolutionary history.

## Conservation and ecosystem services

Ecological knowledge underpins the assessment and management of natural resources. The IUCN Red List has evaluated more than $160,000$ species, of which about $40,000$ are classified as threatened, roughly a quarter of those assessed, and extinction rates are estimated to run hundreds of times the natural background rate. Services such as water regulation, soil conservation and carbon capture are valued globally at about $30$ trillion to $45$ trillion dollars a year, so land development and carbon markets both require quantified assessment.
