## Overview

**Geometry** studies shapes, sizes, positions and the spatial relationships between objects, making it the oldest and most visual branch of mathematics. From the axioms of Euclid to the curvature geometry of Riemann, the subject keeps extending our ability to describe space.

## Euclidean geometry and its axioms

Euclid's *Elements*, compiled around 300 BCE, set out planar geometry axiomatically. Its fifth postulate, that through a point not on a line there is exactly one parallel line, was treated as an unprovable given for roughly two millennia. From the axioms follow the $180$ degree sum of triangle angles, the Pythagorean relation $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ and the constant ratio of circumference to diameter, written $\pi \approx 3.14159$ in modern notation.

## Analytic geometry and area

Descartes represented points as ordered pairs $(x, y)$ and expressed curves as functions $y = f(x)$ whose areas are computed by definite integration. Common figures follow these formulas.

| Figure | Area | Perimeter or surface area |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Circle | $\pi r^2$ | $2\pi r$ |
| Triangle | $\frac{1}{2}bh$ | $a + b + c$ |
| Rectangle | $ab$ | $2(a + b)$ |
| Sphere | — | $4\pi r^2$ |

A sphere of radius $r$ has volume $\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$ whereas Earth, with a mean radius of about $6371$ km, has a surface area near $5.1 \times 10^8$ square kilometres.

## Non-Euclidean geometry and curvature

Gauss, Riemann and Bolyai showed that the fifth postulate could be denied without contradiction, yielding consistent non-Euclidean geometries in which triangle angles sum to more or less than $180$ degrees. Gaussian curvature $K$ is positive on a sphere, zero on a plane and negative on a hyperboloid. In his 1854 lecture Riemann proposed viewing surfaces as manifolds of $n$ dimensions, a geometric language that later became the basis of general relativity.
