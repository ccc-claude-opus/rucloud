## Overview

**Jurisprudence**, also called legal philosophy, examines the nature, validity and legitimacy of law and the methods of interpreting it. It addresses prior questions such as what law is; without answers to them, the interpretation of individual provisions loses its footing.

## Three major traditions

The natural law tradition holds that law must accord with a reason or justice principle standing above the legislator, with Grotius and Kant as representative figures. Legal positivism maintains that validity depends only on social facts of enactment and recognition, not on morality, and names Hobbes, Bentham and Weber among its exponents. Critical legal studies treat law as an artefact of ruling-class ideology, drawing on Marx and Carl Schmitt.

## The positivist–natural law tension

The conflict sharpened after the Second World War, when Nazi law was treated as valid but unjust, forcing jurisprudence to separate validity from legitimacy. Radbruch's 1949 formula held that justice prevails over law when the two collide, and Alexy later formalised this as the non-formality argument. Fuller took the opposite route, listing eight procedural principles requiring law to be general, promulgated, non-retroactive, clear, consistent, possible to obey, reasonably stable and administered consistently.

## Interpretive jurisprudence

Interpretive jurisprudence asks how the meaning of a legal text is established. Arthur Schmitt argues for interpretation starting from the literal text, informed by legislative purpose, a moderate version of semantic method; Radbruch and Alexy stress that usage and argumentative structure matter more than wording. This approach became mainstream in European legal philosophy after the 1960s and shaped the development of principles-based reasoning in German law.

## Orientations compared

| Orientation | Core proposition | Representative figures |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Natural law | Law must conform to justice or reason | Grotius, Kant |
| Legal positivism | Validity rests on social facts of enactment | Hobbes, Bentham, Weber |
| Critical legal studies | Law reflects ruling-class ideology | Marx, Carl Schmitt |
| Interpretive jurisprudence | Meaning is fixed by argument, not wording | Arthur Schmitt, Radbruch |
