## Overview

**Strategy** is a set of choices an organisation makes about its long-term direction and resource allocation. It answers questions about where to compete and how to win, which is a different matter from short-term operational efficiency.

## Levels and frameworks of strategy

Strategy operates at three levels: corporate strategy decides which businesses to enter and how capital is allocated; business strategy decides how to win within an industry; functional strategy turns those goals into marketing, finance and other functional plans. External analysis commonly uses Porter's five forces — rivalry, entrants, substitutes, suppliers and buyers — to judge industry profitability, while internal analysis applies the resource-based view to ask whether the firm holds resources that are hard to imitate.

## Positioning and resource choices

The classic BCG growth-share matrix uses a 10 % market growth line and a relative market share of 1.0 to sort businesses into stars, cash cows, question marks and dogs; weighted by market value, portfolio growth is $ g = \sum_i w_i g_i $. Modern strategy stresses dynamic capability: TSMC's capital expenditure of roughly 30 billion US dollars in 2024, spent expanding advanced-node capacity, shows a long-term position turned into concrete capital allocation.

## Main strategy tools

| Tool | Core question | Typical output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Five forces | Where does industry profit come from and who takes it? | Judgement of industry attractiveness |
| SWOT | How do internal strengths and weaknesses pair with external opportunities and threats? | Strategic direction |
| Value chain | Where is value created and where is cost wasted? | Sources of cost advantage and differentiation |
| Balanced scorecard | How do finance, customers, internal processes and learning connect? | Performance measurement and vision communication |
| Scenario planning | How do we keep options open under uncertainty? | Contingencies and trigger points |
