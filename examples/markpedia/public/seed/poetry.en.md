## Overview

**Poetry** is a literary form that uses concentrated language and rhythm to convey feeling, image, and thought. It is typically set in discrete lines, shaped by meter and repetition so that it can be read aloud, and has moved between oral song and written text for millennia.

## Constituent features

Three features usually mark poetry: division into lines, a rhythmic pattern, and compression of meaning, though no poem needs all three. Free verse drops fixed rhyme, while regulated verse fixes syllable count and rhyme position: a five-character regulated poem runs $8$ lines and $40$ characters, a quatrain $4$ lines and $20$ characters, grouped in four couplets. Chinese regulated verse draws rhyme from the $106$ categories of the Pingshui table, mostly level-tone rhymes.

## Principal forms

Poetic form has shifted with language and circulation. The Chinese Book of Songs collects $305$ poems in three classes — feng, ya, and song — and remains the earliest anthology; ci poems follow fixed tunes, as in 《念奴嬌》, a double stanza of $100$ characters; Yuan dynasty qu fill their own tune names. In the West the sonnet, the epic, and modern free verse mark comparable stages.

| Form | Feature | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Guxing | Irregular line lengths, free of metre | Li Bai, 《將進酒》 |
| Regulated verse | $8$ lines or $4$ lines, rhymed on level tones | Du Fu, 《春望》 |
| Ci | Fixed tune pattern, mixed line lengths | Su Shi, 《念奴嬌·赤壁懷古》 |
| Qu | Suite plus tune name, strongly lyrical | Ma Zhiyuan, 《天淨沙·秋思》 |
| Free verse | No fixed metre, common since 1900 | Rilke, 《Herbsttag》 |

## Oral, ritual, and performed

Many poetic forms grew out of ritual and song: the song sections of the Book of Songs are ancestral temple hymns, and Homeric epics were composed for recitation, the Iliad running to $24$ books and $15,693$ lines against the Odyssey’ $24$ books and $12,110$ lines. The recited tradition stays alive in rock, rap, and folk song, which keeps poetry both a written work and a performed one.
