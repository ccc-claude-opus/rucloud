## Overview

**Plate tectonics** is the theory that Earth's lithosphere is broken into rigid plates that drift relative to one another at rates of a few centimetres per year. It accounts for the distribution of earthquakes, volcanoes, mountain belts and ocean trenches, and forms the backbone of modern Earth science.

## Lithosphere and plates

The lithosphere varies between oceanic and continental types and averages about $100$ km in thickness, floating on the viscous asthenosphere. Seven large plates and several dozen smaller ones are recognised. Most boundaries lie underwater and cannot be visited directly, so they are inferred from earthquake distributions, volcanic island arcs and the shape of the seafloor.

## Three kinds of boundary

Boundaries are classified by relative motion. Divergent boundaries occur mainly along mid-ocean ridges, where plates separate and new oceanic crust forms. Convergent boundaries involve one plate subducting beneath another, producing trenches, volcanic arcs and mountain belts, and the deepest earthquakes there reach source depths of $700$ km. Transform boundaries accommodate sideways slip along faults such as the San Andreas Fault in California.

| Boundary type | Relative motion | Landform result | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Divergent | Plates separate | Ridge, rift valley, shallow quakes | Mid-Atlantic Ridge |
| Convergent | Plates approach | Trench, volcanic arc, orogeny | Mariana Trench |
| Transform | Sideways slip | Strike-slip fault, seismic belt | San Andreas Fault |

## Rates and evidence

Plates move at roughly $2$ to $10$ cm per year, the Pacific plate being fastest and the African plate slowest. As seafloor spreading proceeds, sediment ages increase symmetrically away from the ridge, and magnetic stripes in the basalt record matching reversals on either side. About nine tenths of the largest earthquakes occur at plate boundaries, with the Circum-Pacific belt the most active of all.
