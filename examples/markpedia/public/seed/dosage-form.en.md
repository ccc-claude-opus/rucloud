## Overview

A **dosage form** is the final physical form in which a medicine is presented, such as a tablet, capsule or injection. It determines the route of administration, the speed of absorption, the time to onset and how readily a patient will take it, and it shapes the concentration-time curve.

## Dosage forms and routes

Common solid forms include tablets, capsules, granules, suppositories and powders; semi-solids include ointments and creams; liquids include oral solutions, injections and eye drops. Among routes, oral dosing is the most convenient but the most variable in absorption, intravenous dosing reaches $100$ % bioavailability with the fastest onset, and subcutaneous or intramuscular routes depend on solubility. Eye drops of about $50\,\mu\mathrm{L}$ are dispensed per drop, so the nasolacrimal duct should be compressed for roughly $1$ minute to limit systemic absorption.

## Release design and bioavailability

To shape the concentration-time curve, medicines are made as immediate-release, **modified-release**, enteric-coated or sublingual preparations. Controlled-release forms rely on matrices or osmotic pumps and suit drugs with short half-lives that still need sustained concentrations. **Bioavailability** $F$ is the fraction of a dose reaching systemic circulation; oral drugs are limited by first-pass metabolism, so $F$ may fall to around $20$ %. Changing dosage form also changes $F$ and peak concentration, so **different dosage forms are not interchangeable dose for dose**.

## Dose conversion and dosing interval

Concentration and volume conversions follow $C_1 V_1 = C_2 V_2$, and clinicians must be fluent in common units: $1$ g = $1\,000$ mg, $1$ kg $\approx 2.2$ lb, with infants and renal impairment often dosed by body surface area (BSA). Maintenance dose follows $MD = \frac{C_{ss} \times CL \times \tau}{F}$ and a loading dose follows $LD = \frac{V_d \times C_{ss}}{F}$, Dosing intervals are usually set at $2$ to $3$ half-lives so that fluctuations remain inside the therapeutic window.

| Dosage form | Release behaviour | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Immediate-release tablet | Rapid dissolution | Need for fast onset |
| Modified-release tablet | Near zero-order release | Sustained concentration |
| Enteric-coated tablet | Stable in gastric acid | Protecting drug or gastric mucosa |
| Sublingual tablet | Absorbed across mucosa | Rapid symptom relief |
| Suppository | Rectal absorption | Unable to swallow or prone to vomiting |
