## Overview

A **landform** is a natural feature of the Earth's surface produced by the long interaction of internal and external forces — plains, mountain ranges, plateaus and basins. Landforms condition where people settle, how rivers run, and where soils and transport routes develop.

## Principal types

Landforms are commonly grouped as plains, mountains, plateaus, basins, hills and valleys. Land covers about $149$ million square kilometres, with a mean elevation near $875$ m, well above sea level, which is why most rivers run to the ocean rather than to inland basins. The table below lists the diagnostic traits of the major types.

| Type | Diagnostic features | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plain | Low, gently graded, thick deposits | Amazon plain, European plain |
| Mountain | Raised by folding or faulting, steep and deeply dissected | Himalaya, Andes |
| Plateau | Broad high surface with abrupt edges | Brazilian Highlands, Tibetan Plateau |
| Basin | Low floor enclosed by higher ground | Great Basin, Congo Basin |

## Measuring elevation

The highest point is the summit of Everest in the Himalayas, at $8848.86$ m as jointly declared in $2020$, while the deepest lies in the Challenger Deep of the Mariana Trench, over $10900$ m below sea level. The lowest point on land is the shore of the Dead Sea, some $430$ m below sea level. These figures are cross-checked with satellite radar altimetry and shipborne echo sounding.

## Causes and evolution

Internal forces such as subduction, continental collision and volcanism supply the skeleton, while weathering, river erosion, transport and deposition continually rework it. Rivers work by headward erosion, cutting through divides and building deltas downstream. Long-weathered mountains lose height and gentler slope to become hills or plains: the Himalaya still rises by about $10$ mm a year while rivers cut into it faster still, and over millions of years such ranges are worn down to low hills.
