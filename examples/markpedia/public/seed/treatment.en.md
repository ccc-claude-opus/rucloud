## Overview

**Treatment** covers the interventions and care used against a disease, spanning drugs, surgery, radiation, rehabilitation and lifestyle change. Its effect depends not only on the intervention itself but also on how the goal is set, how strong the evidence is, and how well it matches individual risk.

## Layers of therapeutic goal

Treatment is layered by intent. **Curative** therapy aims to eliminate the cause, such as surgical tumour removal. **Symptomatic** therapy relieves complaints without changing the course, **supportive** therapy replaces lost function such as dialysis in renal failure, and **palliative** therapy targets quality of life rather than survival. A single disease moves between these layers over time: breast cancer may progress from curative surgery to hormonal and targeted maintenance, and finally to hospice care.

## The effect of non-pharmacological therapy

Non-drug interventions are often underestimated although their effect sizes rival those of medication. Accumulating at least $150$ minutes of moderate-intensity exercise per week lowers systolic blood pressure by roughly $5$ to $8$ mmHg, and a weight loss of $5$ % to $10$ % is usually enough to improve glycaemic control in type 2 diabetes. Smoking cessation brings lung cancer mortality close to that of never-smokers within $20$ years.

## Guidelines and strength of evidence

Clinical recommendations usually follow the **GRADE** system, in which randomised trials yield strong recommendations, observational studies yield conditional ones, and expert opinion ranks lowest. Every recommendation balances benefit against adverse effects and cost, so the net value of, say, antihypertensive treatment rises with baseline risk. When the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) is smaller than the measurement error of the instrument, a change in numbers need not mean a change the patient can actually feel.

## Treatment patterns and shared decisions

Acute, stable and remissive phases call for different strategies, and chronic disease is commonly managed by **step-up** or **step-down** therapy: start with one approach and escalate only when it proves insufficient or poorly tolerated. Where several reasonable options exist, shared decision making requires presenting the evidence in a form the patient understands and folding in their values and living conditions; high-risk or invasive procedures additionally require explicit informed consent.

| Layer | Main purpose | Typical examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Curative | Remove pathogen or lesion | Surgical resection, eradication antibiotics |
| Symptomatic | Relieve complaints | Antipyretics, bronchodilators |
| Supportive | Maintain organ function | Dialysis, transfusion, oxygen therapy |
| Palliative | Improve quality of life | Pain control, hospice medicine |
