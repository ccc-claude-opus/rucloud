## Overview

A **statute** is a rule of law enacted and promulgated by a legislative body through a formal procedure, and it is the main source of law in most legal systems. Statutes fix rights, duties and legal consequences in text, in contrast to case law, which has to be worked out case by case.

## Enactment and promulgation

A statute passes through initiation, deliberation, voting and promulgation before it becomes binding. Article 175 of the Constitution provides that a bill passed by the Legislative Yuan in three readings is promulgated by the President, while the Central Regulatory Standards Act divides central regulations into statutes, orders and administrative rules. Only statutes must go through the legislature; regulations normally take effect on the date of promulgation, though the issuing agency may set a later date to allow for preparation.

## Anatomy of a provision

Laws are divided hierarchically into books, chapters, sections, articles, paragraphs, subparagraphs and items, with the article as the smallest operative unit. Article 1 of the Civil Code — "Civil law has no effect unless it is enacted by statute or by an order authorized by statute" — shows the standard structure of subject, condition and legal consequence: the condition comes first, the consequence of invalidity follows.

## Hierarchy and validity

| Level | How it is made | Relation of validity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Constitution | Amendment procedure (legislature or referendum) | Highest authority; all other norms must comply |
| Statute | Three readings in the Legislative Yuan | Prevails over orders |
| Order | Issued by an administrative agency on statutory authority or inherent power | May not conflict with superior law |
| Administrative rule | Issued by an administrative agency on its own authority | Externally binding on the public |

## Amendment and constitutional review

Laws are amended either wholesale or in parts, and amendments follow the same three-reading and promulgation path. New rules normally do not act retroactively unless the text says so; exceptions include the criminal principle that the older, more lenient provision may be chosen by the accused. Whether a statute violates the Constitution is reviewed by the Justices of the Supreme Court in the Constitutional Court, which handed down its first judgment on 28 October 2022.
