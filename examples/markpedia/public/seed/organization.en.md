## Overview

**Organization** is the structure through which people, authority and processes are arranged to pursue common goals. It determines how information flows, who answers to whom, and therefore how fast and creatively the organisation can act.

## Types of structure

Three basic structural forms recur: functional structures group people by task, divisional structures by product or region, and matrix structures combine both. Each added layer of hierarchy lengthens the distance information must travel, so span of control — the number of people a supervisor manages directly — is usually kept to 5 to 8, and headcount must satisfy $ N \leq n \times h $, where $ N $ is the number of employees and $ h $ the number of levels. Matrix designs share resources well but often produce conflict over dual reporting.

## Transaction costs and informal networks

Coase argued in 1937 that firms exist because market transaction costs exceed internal coordination costs: when an activity requires specific investment and close supervision, running it inside the organisation is cheaper. Granovetter showed in 1973 that weak ties carry information efficiently, demonstrating that a layer of informal networks operates alongside formal authority to make execution actually happen.

## Elements of organisational design

| Element | Core question | Common practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Division of labour | How is work split? | By function, process or customer group |
| Authority hierarchy | Who decides and who executes? | Functional bureaucracy, teams, collegial boards |
| Communication channels | How does information travel? | Formal documents, regular meetings, digital platforms |
| Incentive mechanisms | How is effort sustained? | Pay, bonuses, career progression |
| Bases of power | Where does authority come from? | Position, expertise, participation |
