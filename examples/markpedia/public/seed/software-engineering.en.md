## Overview

**Software engineering** is the disciplined method for turning requirements into deployable, maintainable software, spanning requirements, design, implementation, testing, deployment and operations. What separates it from simply writing code is a repeatable, measurable process that keeps cost, schedule and quality under simultaneous control.

## How the discipline formed

Software engineering took shape in the 1970s. In The Mythical Man-Month (1972), Frederick P. Brooks documented the classic failure of adding staff to a late project: a schedule of 12 months stretched to 18 months, later called Brooks's law. From the 1990s onward, agile development, continuous integration (CI) and continuous deployment (CD) pushed short, feedback-driven cycles into mainstream practice.

## Stages, artifacts and verification

Modern practice splits the work into requirements, architecture, implementation, verification and operations, each with defined artifacts and exit criteria — for example traceable use cases on the requirements side and consistent interface contracts on the design side. Teams commonly allocate automated tests along a test pyramid: roughly 70 % unit tests, 20 % integration tests and 10 % end-to-end tests, so most defects surface where they are cheapest to fix.

## Metrics used to judge engineering performance

Recent fast-and-reliable-delivery practice has replaced vague statements such as "shipped on time" with a small set of measurable indicators. The metrics must balance speed against stability, since any one of them in isolation rewards short-term behaviour. The table below lists four common delivery metrics and reference values.

| Metric | Definition | Reference value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deployment frequency | Number of releases to production | Several per day to once per week |
| Lead time for changes | Time from commit to production | Measured in minutes or hours |
| Change failure rate | Share of changes causing a rollback or incident | Below 5 % is considered good |
| Mean time to restore | Time from outage to full recovery | Measured in minutes |
