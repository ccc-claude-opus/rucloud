## Overview

A **political system** is the institutional arrangement through which state power is acquired, distributed and exercised. It determines who decides, how legitimacy is obtained and how decisions are enforced, and it is the central unit of analysis in comparative politics.

## Acquiring and exercising power

A political system has three layers: how power is granted (election, heredity, revolution or appointment), how it is divided among institutions (legislature, executive, judiciary, defence), and how citizens influence its exercise (participation channels, interest groups, the press). The difference between democracy and authoritarianism lies not in whether elections exist but in whether competition is genuine, whether opposition can exist legally, and whether rules bind rulers as well as citizens.

## Main types of system

| Type | Access to power | How power is exercised |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Liberal democracy | Regular competitive elections | Alternation of power, multiparty competition, rule of law |
| Electoral democracy | Elections with restricted competition | Multiparty in form, narrow participation in practice |
| Authoritarian | Power monopolised by a governing group | Rests on the military, bureaucracy or a single party |
| Totalitarian | Highly mobilised single leadership | State reaches into every layer of society |

## Separation of powers and checks

The idea descends from Locke and Montesquieu, who in The Spirit of the Laws of 1748 treated the separation of legislature, executive and judiciary as a safeguard against despotism. Modern systems mostly adopt a version of division without antagonism: courts review legislation through constitutional review, so supreme courts and constitutional courts hold the final say over the validity of laws. Switzerland's referendum practice returns a share of power directly to citizens, supplementing rather than opposing representative rule.

## Measuring systems in practice

Freedom House assesses close to 200 countries and territories in its annual Freedom in the World report, with about 84 rated free in the latest round and the remainder partly free or not free. Such indices show that countries meeting strict liberal criteria remain a small minority, and that political freedom and rule of law fall well short in most of the countries assessed.
