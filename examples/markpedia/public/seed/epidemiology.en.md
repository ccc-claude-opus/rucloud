## Overview

**Epidemiology** studies the distribution and determinants of disease and health in populations. By measurement and study design it estimates risk and supplies the evidence base for public health decisions.

## Scope and branches

In its broad sense epidemiology also covers interventions that promote health and prevent disease. By purpose it divides into **descriptive** work, which maps how disease is distributed in a population, **analytical** work that tests hypotheses through case-control and cohort designs, and **experimental** work such as randomised controlled trials. Where clinical medicine concentrates on the management of an individual patient, epidemiology works at the population level on risk and causal inference, so the same data can be read differently under different designs.

## Core measures

**Incidence** measures the rate of new cases and **prevalence** the proportion of existing cases at a point in time; roughly $P = I \times D$, where $D$ is the average duration of disease, which is why a chronic condition can have high prevalence despite a modest incidence. The relative risk (RR) and odds ratio (OR) are the common effect measures of analytical studies, with the relative risk defined as the incidence in the exposed divided by the incidence in the unexposed.

| Measure | Formula | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Incidence | New cases / person-time | Causation, risk prediction |
| Prevalence | Cases / population | Health service planning |
| Relative risk | $RR = I_e / I_u$ | Strength of the exposure-disease link |
| Odds ratio | $OR = ad/bc$ | Case-control studies |
| Case fatality rate | Deaths / cases | Severity and quality of care |

## Study designs and interpretation

Case-control studies look back from outcome to exposure and suit rare diseases or long latency periods, but are prone to recall bias; cohort studies follow exposed groups forward and allow incidence and relative risks to be computed directly. Both must handle **confounding**: smoking relates to lung cancer and to cardiovascular disease, so failing to adjust overstates its cardiovascular effect. Most observational findings express association rather than causation, and reading them properly means weighing dose-response and temporal order.
