## Overview

A **precedent** is a court decision whose reasoning later cases may rely on. In common law systems it is a source of law alongside, and often above, legislation, because it turns abstract rules into concrete guidance for courts and litigants.

## The scope of binding force

In the United States, a Supreme Court decision binds every federal and state court, while a state supreme court binds only its own lower courts and merely persuades elsewhere. English courts follow stare decisis, though the House of Lords gained power to overrule itself after London Street Tramways in 1932. The US Supreme Court receives roughly 7,000 petitions for certiorari each year and typically decides fewer than 100 of them.

## How the two legal families use precedent

In civil law systems judgments are not formally binding, yet similar mechanisms have developed: China's Supreme People's Court has issued guiding cases since 2010 that lower courts are expected to follow, and Japan's Supreme Court has established that its judgments bind. Taiwan's Supreme Court publishes the key holdings extracted from its decisions, giving precedent practical weight without making it formal statute.

## Holding and dictum

The binding part of a decision is the holding, the proposition necessary to dispose of the case; everything else is dictum, persuasive but not obligatory. The distinction, set out by Frederic King in 1897, is the most technical problem in case law and the common reason a perfectly sound sentence from an earlier case loses a case.

## Following, distinguishing and overruling

A court confronted with an earlier authority has three options:

| Action | Term | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Follow | follow | Takes the precedent as the basis of the decision |
| Distinguish | distinguish | Finds a factual difference making the precedent inapplicable |
| Overrule | overrule | Denies the precedent's authority altogether |

Distinguishing is the most common route, since it preserves an existing principle while accommodating new facts; overruling is costlier and requires the court to explain why the old rule no longer fits.
