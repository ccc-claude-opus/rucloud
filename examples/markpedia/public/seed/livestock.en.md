## Overview

**Livestock farming** raises animals for meat, milk, eggs, wool and draft power. Modern systems depend heavily on feed crops and veterinary technology, supplying roughly two fifths of the animal protein eaten worldwide while raising questions about emissions and antibiotic use.

## Herd sizes and species

Pigs, chickens, cattle, sheep and goats dominate world livestock keeping. In 2022 the world held roughly $33$ billion chickens, $800$ million pigs, $1.5$ billion cattle, $1.3$ billion sheep and $1.1$ billion goats, with chickens far more numerous because of their rapid turnover. Holding systems fall into grazing, semi-grazing and intensive confinement, and the choice determines animal space, feed sources, manure handling, unit cost and the environmental intensity of output.

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Chicken | 33 billion | Meat, eggs | Laying cycle about 300 days |

## Output and industry scale

World meat production reached about $370$ million tonnes in 2023, of which poultry contributed some $145$ million tonnes, pig meat $124$ million tonnes and bovine meat $78$ million tonnes. Global milk output approached $1$ billion tonnes over the same period, and egg production was roughly $80$ to $90$ million tonnes. Yield gains come from breeding, ration formulation and disease control; a high-producing dairy cow can yield $8,000$ to $12,000$ kg of milk a year.

## Feed and resource efficiency

Feed is the largest cost and the largest environmental burden in livestock production, and about one third of world grain output is fed to animals. Feed conversion ratio is the standard comparison between species: chickens need about $2$ kg of feed for $1$ kg of meat, pigs $3$ to $4$ kg, and cattle $6$ to $8$ kg, while milk and eggs fall between poultry and pork in efficiency. Ranking systems based on protein output alone therefore produce different conclusions from those based on meat alone.

## Environmental and health issues

Livestock supply chains account for roughly $12$ % to $14$ % of global greenhouse gas emissions, mainly through methane from ruminants and manure management; the life-cycle footprint of beef runs about $25$ to $60$ kg of carbon dioxide equivalent per kilogram, far above chicken or legumes. Veterinary drug use also raises the risk of antimicrobial resistance, and animal welfare in intensive housing has become a growing focus of regulation and consumer demand, pushing precision feeding and low-emission rations to the centre of research.
