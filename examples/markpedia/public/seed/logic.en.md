## Overview

**Logic** studies the form of reasoning and the validity of arguments, asking whether a conclusion must be true when its premises are. It abstracts reasoning from context and turns it into a checkable symbolic structure.

## Deduction and induction

Deduction is characterized by validity rather than truth: when the form is correct, true premises guarantee a true conclusion, though the premises themselves may be true or false. Induction moves from particular cases to a general conclusion that holds only probably, as when $100$ black crows yield the claim that all crows are black — refuted by a single white crow. Hume added that induction cannot be established logically, since it assumes the future will resemble the past.

## Propositional logic and truth

Propositional logic analyzes whole statements joined by and, or, if–then, and not; each statement is either true or false, so a formula with $n$ statements admits $2^n$ truth assignments. Validity can be settled by truth table or expressed through inference rules, with the material conditional captured by $p \to q \equiv \lnot p \lor q$, so that an entire argument reduces to a tautology.

## Milestones in modern logic

In the nineteenth century Boole made logical operations algebraic; in the early twentieth Frege, Russell, and Tarski built formal systems around propositional and predicate calculi and distinguished object language from metalanguage in order to speak about truth. Tarski defined the concept of truth in $1933$, and in $1931$ Gödel showed that any system expressive enough for arithmetic cannot be both consistent and complete — logic’s power and its limits appearing at once.
