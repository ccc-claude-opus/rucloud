## Overview

**Linguistics** is the scientific study of language, covering form, meaning, use, and change. It addresses what kind of system language is and how language stands to mind and to society.

## Major branches

Linguistics distinguishes the linguistics of language from the science of language: in his $1916$ Course in General Linguistics Saussure separated the system (langue) from individual speech acts (parole), fixing the object of structuralist inquiry. The branches are not independent — pragmatics arose precisely because semantic analysis could not explain meaning in conversation.

| Branch | Object of study | Central question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Phonetics | Physical realization of speech | How sounds are produced and heard |
| Phonology | Sound system and oppositions | How phonemes are distributed |
| Grammar | Structure of sentences | How words combine into sentences |
| Semantics | Meaning and reference | How words and sentences convey sense |
| Pragmatics | Language in context | How speakers are understood |

## Typology and data

Ethnologue records roughly $8,300$ living languages, of which only a few dozen are widely spoken, and the great majority have no written corpus at all. Typology classifies languages by structural features rather than by geography or ethnicity, and finds that the possibilities are constrained: word order, case marking, and verb position constrain one another, yielding on the order of several dozen configurations rather than arbitrary ones.

## Schools and methods

Twentieth-century linguistics underwent several shifts of orientation: structuralism, beginning with Saussure, described sound systems and grammars without inferring psychology; generative linguistics after $1957$ treated linguistic competence as a formalizable system; sociolinguistics returned speakers, situations, and relations of power to the centre of analysis, attending to the gap between variation and standardization. Corpus linguistics and experimental phonetics have since supplied new evidence.
