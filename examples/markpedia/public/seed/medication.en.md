## Overview

**Medication** is the principles and practice of using drugs against disease, covering prescribing, dispensing, administration and monitoring. Its central problem is choosing the active ingredient, dose, route, frequency and duration so that an effective and safe concentration is maintained despite individual variation.

## The anatomy of a prescription

A complete prescription carries the generic name, dose, dosage form, route, frequency and duration, and only the generic name identifies an active ingredient unambiguously. Standard abbreviations include `qd` (once daily), `bid` (twice daily), `tid` (three times daily), `prn` (as needed) and `ac` (before meals); reading them correctly is the first line of medication safety. Prescribing also requires recording liver and kidney function, allergy history and every product currently taken.

## Pharmacokinetics

Pharmacokinetics describes what happens to a drug in the body: absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion. The **bioavailability** $F$ of an oral drug is limited by first-pass metabolism in the gut wall and liver, so $F$ may fall well below $1$, whereas intravenous dosing is $100$ %. The half-life $t_{1/2}$ is the time for plasma concentration to halve, given by $t_{1/2} = \frac{0.693 \times V_d}{CL}$ , where $V_d$ is the volume of distribution and $CL$ the clearance.

## Dose conversion and population differences

The therapeutic window separates effective from toxic concentrations, and the narrower it is, the more monitoring is required. Falling renal function causes renally cleared drugs to accumulate, so creatinine clearance is used to adjust dosing through the Cockcroft-Gault formula $CrCl = \frac{(140 - A) \times W}{72 \times S_{cr}}$, where $A$ is age, $W$ weight in kilograms, and females are multiplied by $0.85$. Older adults and patients with impaired liver function are generally managed by starting low and titrating slowly.

| Dilution | $C_1 V_1 = C_2 V_2$ |
| Mass | $1$ g = $1\,000$ mg, $1$ mg = $1\,000$ mcg |
