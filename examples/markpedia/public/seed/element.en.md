## Overview

**An element** is a basic substance defined by a unique atomic number and fixed chemical properties, forming the unit of composition of all matter. To date $118$ elements are recognised, $94$ of them occurring naturally and the rest synthesised as radioactive species.

## Definition and isotopes

An element is defined by its atomic number $Z$ which counts the protons in the nucleus. Atoms sharing the same $Z$ have the same chemical properties, so elements cannot be broken down by chemical means. Atoms of one element may differ in neutron count, giving isotopes such as ${}^{12}C$ and ${}^{13}C$ or ${}^{35}Cl$ and ${}^{37}Cl$ which behave alike chemically but differ in mass and nuclear reaction rates.

## The periodic table and structure

Mendeleev published his periodic table in 1869, and today's $118$ elements follow the periodic law: the period number counts electron shells, so the first period holds only $2$ elements while the seventh holds $32$ elements. The group number reflects valence electrons and therefore chemical behaviour.

| Element | Symbol | Atomic number | Main use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hydrogen | H | 1 | Fuel, ammonia synthesis |
| Carbon | C | 6 | Steelmaking, fuel, carbon fibre |
| Oxygen | O | 8 | Respiration, steelmaking |
| Gold | Au | 79 | Electrical contacts, reserves |
| Uranium | U | 92 | Nuclear fuel |

## Abundance and radioactivity

Elements are distributed very unevenly in the universe: by mass, hydrogen accounts for about $74$ % and helium about $24$ %, together more than $98$ % of all cosmic mass, with heavier elements present only in trace amounts. Radioisotopes have half-lives that make them useful for dating and tracing, for instance ${}^{14}C$ with a half-life of $5730$ years is the archaeological standard, while ${}^{238}U$ has $4.47 \times 10^9$ years and ${}^{235}U$ has $7.04 \times 10^8$ years, the latter being the basis of nuclear energy and weapons.
