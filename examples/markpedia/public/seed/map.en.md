## Overview

A **map** is a representation of geographic information transferred onto a flat surface according to a chosen scale and projection. It is the basic instrument for recording, measuring and conveying spatial information, and the central tool of geography.

## The three elements of a map

Every map needs a scale, a legend and an orientation. Together they determine how much of the world a map can actually convey, and the scale in particular limits the smallest feature that can be recorded.

| Element | Purpose | Common form |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scale | States the reduction factor | Numerical $1:100000$, verbal or bar |
| Legend | Explains the symbols | Colour, line style, point symbols |
| Orientation | Gives direction and grid | North arrow, graticule |

## Projections and distortion

The Earth is nearly spherical, so flattening it always distorts something, and each projection preserves a different property. The Mercator projection of $1569$ is conformal and keeps angles correct, which suits navigation, but it inflates area at high latitude: at $60$ °N the area scale is about $4$ times the true value. The Peters projection of $1973$ is equal-area, sacrificing shape to keep area correct.

## Digital maps and GIS

Digitised maps describe the world either as vectors, points, lines and polygons, or as raster grids of image cells, and a GIS adds spatial analysis such as viewshed, buffer and network analysis. The Global Positioning System locates a receiver to roughly $3$ to $5$ m, while satellite remote sensing allows regular comparison of surface change, and together these keep modern maps current.
