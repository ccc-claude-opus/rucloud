## Overview

A **satellite** is an artificial body orbiting the Earth or another celestial body. Satellites remove the geographic limits of ground stations: an orbit can keep one craft continuously over a region, and a polar orbit sweeps the entire globe each day.

## Orbit families

| Orbit | Altitude | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Low Earth orbit (LEO) | 200–2,000 km | Earth observation, imaging, space stations |
| Medium Earth orbit (MEO) | 2,000–35,786 km | Navigation constellations (GPS) |
| Geostationary orbit (GEO) | 35,786 km | Communications and weather satellites |

## Why altitude determines purpose

Orbital period follows $T = 2\pi\sqrt{a^3 / \mu}$, with $a$ the semi-major axis and $\mu$ the Earth's standard gravitational parameter. A low orbit has a period near 90 minutes, allowing rapid revisits but with a moving coverage swath; a geostationary orbit has a period matching Earth's rotation, so the satellite hangs over one point on the equator.

## Design and station keeping

Satellites must use propellant to counter drag and changes in gravitational potential, so their design life is limited by propellant budget. Most carry thrusters and solar panels, and hold pointing accuracy with star trackers and gyroscopes.
