## Overview

**Urban policy** is the planning and governance of urban land, housing, transport and public space. It becomes most pressing once population concentrates in cities, and it shapes housing costs, commuting efficiency and the urban–rural gap.

## The scale of urbanisation

The UN World Urbanization Prospects puts the share of the global population living in cities at about 56 % in 2024, projected to reach 68 % by 2050, with Asia and Africa growing fastest. Concentration yields economic efficiency, because firms share labour, knowledge and infrastructure, but it also congests housing, land and transport. Urban policy therefore negotiates between the agglomeration dividend and the cost of crowding.

## Planning instruments

| Instrument | Purpose | Common metric |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Zoning | Separate residential, commercial and industrial land | Floor-area ratio, site coverage |
| Housing supply and rental regulation | Stabilise prices and the rental market | Price-to-income ratio, vacancy rate |
| Transit investment and interchange | Improve accessibility of public transport | Public transport mode share |
| Public space and walkability | Improve living quality and safety | Green space per capita, walk distance |
| Urban renewal | Upgrade ageing districts and structural safety | Renewal rate, density bonuses |

## Transport and housing

Transport policy sets the practical scale of a city. Transit-oriented development concentrates homes and jobs around stations, narrowing the gap between commuting distance and straight-line distance; in Taipei the public transport mode share has stayed near 40 % and metro ridership exceeds 2 million trips a day. Housing policy works on supply, through zoning, density bonuses and public housing, and on demand, through rental rules and vacancy taxes, shaping affordability.

## Governance challenges

Urban policy runs into three structural difficulties: fragmented jurisdiction, with roads, railways, operations and land held by separate agencies; fiscal mismatch, as local revenues cannot support infrastructure-intensive cities; and short political cycles, since major projects span several terms of office. Many municipalities respond with cross-agency urban governance platforms and judge performance by long-term indicators rather than single projects.
