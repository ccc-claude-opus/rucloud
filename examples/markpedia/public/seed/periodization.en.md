## Overview

**Periodization** divides a long span of time into stages so that the past can be narrated and compared. Each generation draws its dividing lines afresh, and a new period means a new judgment about which changes matter.

## Criteria for dividing time

Periodization usually rests on three kinds of criteria: political (dynastic change, shifts in regime), socioeconomic (modes of production and class structure), and cultural-technological (main currents of thought and material technique). The same span yields different schemes when criteria differ — Chinese history by dynasty divides into four periods, while a scheme keyed to the rise of European cities yields a quite different frame.

## Common boundary markers

Markers are mostly political events or institutional changes, so they carry the emphases of their own moment: the French Revolution reshaped European periodization, while the fall of empires and the Renaissance marked another tradition. Chinese historiography instead counts by reign titles and dynasties, with a single ruler’s reign often lasting only a few decades.

| Period | Start | Marker |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Antiquity | 476 BCE | Traditional endpoint of the classical age in European history |
| Middle Ages | 5th century BCE to 1453 | From the classical world to the fall of Constantinople |
| Renaissance | from 1453 | Revival of classical letters and spread of printing |
| Modern | from 1789 | The French Revolution and industrialization |
| Contemporary | from 1945 | End of the Second World War to the present |

## Controversy and consequences

Periodization is never purely technical: the markers chosen decide which groups and which events fall on the progressive side. Debates in Chinese historiography over feudalism in the Western Zhou versus the Warring States spring from differing periods producing opposite verdicts on social character. Fernand Braudel of the Annales school proposed the longue durée, stressing that structural change spans centuries or millennia and unsettling the habit of organizing history by event.
