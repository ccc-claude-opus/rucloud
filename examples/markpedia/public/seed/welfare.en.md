## Overview

**Welfare policy** comprises the arrangements a state makes for social security, income protection and the support of vulnerable groups. It spans social insurance, social assistance and social services, and expresses how responsibility is divided between government and market.

## Three tiers and types of welfare regime

Social insurance exchanges contributions for benefits, as in pension, health and employment-injury schemes; social assistance tops up the incomes of those below a defined threshold; social services meet needs through education, care and housing. Esping-Andersen's 1990 classification distinguishes liberal, conservative-corporatist and social democratic regimes along two dimensions, decommodification and market orientation: liberal systems merely provide a floor, while social democratic ones replace family and market responsibility with public provision.

## Expenditure levels and financing

Social expenditure in OECD countries averages about 20 % of GDP, with pensions and health taking the largest shares and creating the bulk of fiscal pressure. How it is financed determines fairness: tax-funded benefits are decoupled from contributions and thus socialised, whereas employer and employee contributions link benefits to contribution years, favouring those with longer work records. Many states mix the two and adjust replacement rates to contain long-term spending.

## Integrating health and long-term care

Differences in health coverage appear mainly on the financing and supply side. Universal insurance pools risk across the whole population, avoiding the gaps that arise when coverage is tied to employment status and leaves elderly and non-working people exposed; Taiwan's National Health Insurance covered about 20.4 million people in 2023, about 99 % of the eligible population. In long-term care, insurance-based and tax-funded models each have advocates, and both face shortages in the workforce and complex eligibility assessment.

## Evaluation and controversy

| Issue | Argument for | Main criticism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Universalism | Avoids stigma and exclusion | Dilutes available resources |
| Cash benefits | Respect recipients' autonomy | Real purchasing power may not rise |
| In-kind services | Meet needs directly | High administrative cost, hard quality control |
| Employer contributions | Link protection to labour contribution | Does not reach the self-employed |
