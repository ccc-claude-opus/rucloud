## Overview

A **tide** is the periodic rise and fall of sea level produced by the gravitational pull of the Moon and the Sun, on a cycle of roughly half a day. Tides are the most regular motion in the ocean, shaping navigation and fishing while storing a renewable source of energy.

## Cause and period

The tidal force of the Moon is about $2.2$ times that of the Sun, which is less powerful but far closer to the Earth. Because the Earth's rotation and the Moon's revolution differ, one tidal day lasts $24$ hours and $50$ minutes, so successive high tides are separated by about $12$ hours and $25$ minutes. This is the lunar day.

## Tide types and range

According to the spacing of high tides and the variation in range, tides are classified as semidiurnal, diurnal or mixed, and most coasts experience mixed tides. Range is amplified in funnel-shaped estuaries, while southwestern Taiwan typically sees only $1$ to $2$ m.

| Type | Interval between highs | Character | Representative area |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Semidiurnal | about $12.4$ h | Two highs a day, similar ranges | North Sea, west England |
| Diurnal | about $24.8$ h | One high tide a day | Northern Gulf of Mexico |
| Mixed | In between | Irregular spacing, varying range | Most of Taiwan and Japan |
| Large range | Site dependent | Ranges above $10$ m | Bay of Fundy, Qiantang |

## Spring tides, neap tides and uses

At new and full moon the Sun, Earth and Moon lie nearly in line, so the two tidal forces reinforce and the range reaches a maximum known as spring tides. At the quarter moons the forces act at right angles and the range is smallest, giving neap tides, with the two alternating roughly every fortnight. The energy in tidal flow can be captured by turbines in bays and fjords, where a large range and fast currents yield high output: plants at Annapolis in Canada and Sihwa in South Korea use turbines rated in hundreds of kilowatts up to more than $1$ MW.
