## Overview

**Genetic engineering** is the deliberate, site-specific modification of a genome using molecular biology techniques, inserting, deleting or editing sequences to give an organism new traits. It is now routine in crop breeding, medicine and industrial enzyme production.

## Historical development

The discovery of restriction enzymes and DNA ligase in the 1970s made in vitro DNA recombination possible. The first transgenic plants were produced in 1983, a genetically modified tomato reached the market in 1994 as the first commercialized GM food, and insect-resistant maize was planted on a large scale in the United States from 1996. The CRISPR-Cas9 system, published in 2012, lowered the barrier to editing so sharply that precise substitutions and simultaneous multi-gene edits are now routine.

## Applications in crops

Engineered crops mainly target insect resistance, herbicide tolerance, virus resistance and stress tolerance. Bt maize and Bt cotton together covered more than $70$ million hectares in 2019, and virus-resistant papaya revived Hawaii's papaya industry after the devastating virus outbreak of the late 1990s. The genome-edited rice known as Golden Rice aims to raise beta-carotene content in the grain.

| Application | Example | Benefit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Insect resistance | Bt maize, Bt cotton | Fewer insecticide applications |
| Herbicide tolerance | Glyphosate-tolerant maize | Simpler weed management |
| Virus resistance | Papaya ringspot virus resistant papaya | Saved a collapsing industry |
| Nutrition | Golden rice, high-vitamin tomato | Greater micronutrient density |

## Medical and industrial applications

In medicine the field centers on gene therapy, cell therapy and recombinant proteins. The first approved gene therapy, for RPE65 retinal disease, arrived in 2012; the first CAR-T cell therapies were approved for leukemia and lymphoma in 2017; and mRNA vaccines were used at scale during the COVID-19 pandemic of 2020. Industrially, recombinant insulin reached the market in 1982 as the first commercial gene-engineered drug.

## Regulation and risk management

Regulation of engineered products follows one of two models: a substantive-equivalence approach, under which an engineered organism may be exempted if it differs negligibly from conventional counterparts, or a process-based approach requiring case-by-case review and labeling. Debates center on the evolution of herbicide-tolerant crops and resistant weeds, effects on non-target organisms, and labeling rules for edited crops. The prevailing scientific view is that marketed engineered crops are as safe as conventional ones, with the main risks residing in oversight and enforcement.
