## Overview

**Structural engineering** is the discipline of designing load-bearing structures so that they remain stable and safe under load and environment. Its output is a design for strength, stiffness, toughness and durability, applied to buildings, bridges, dams, towers and port structures.

## Design methods and safety

Two approaches dominate structural design. Allowable stress design (ASD) compares service loads directly with allowable material stresses, while load and resistance factor design (LRFD) multiplies loads by load factors and material strengths by resistance factors $ \phi $ before comparison. LRFD was fully adopted in the United States steel codes around 1990 and is now primary in most codes, targeting a reliability index of about $ \beta \approx 3.0 $ at the strength limit state.

## Load categories and design values

Loads fall into dead loads such as self-weight, variable loads from people, equipment and vehicles, and environmental loads from wind, earthquake and temperature. Typical European code figures for variable loads are about 2.0 kN/m² for offices, 2.5 kN/m² for car parks and up to 5.0 kN/m² for heavy vehicle areas. The 21 September 1999 Chi-Chi earthquake in Taiwan, magnitude 7.3, killed 2,297 people and damaged more than 50,000 buildings, prompting substantial revisions to seismic design codes.

## Structural materials and their mechanical properties

Material choice follows the type of loading: concrete performs well in compression but barely in tension, so steel bars supply the missing tension; steel offers ductility and weldability, which suits members requiring toughness; timber and bamboo compete on low weight and local availability. The table below summarises typical strengths and restrictions.

| Material | Typical strength | Characteristics and limits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reinforced concrete | 21 – 28 MPa in compression, 3 – 5 MPa in tension | Weak in tension, needs steel reinforcement |
| Structural steel | Yield strength 250 – 460 MPa | Ductile, weldable, high modulus |
| Timber | 20 – 40 MPa compression parallel to grain | Strongly anisotropic, needs moisture and pest protection |
| Bamboo | 40 – 60 MPa in compression | Low density, fast growing, limited supply |
| Prestressed concrete | Several hundred to several thousand kN of axial force per tendon | Controls cracking, suits long spans |
